\title{Evidence Coverage and the Cost of False Isolation: Verifying
Malicious Beacon Nodes in Mobile Ad Hoc Networks}


%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
%% ABSTRACT  (replace whole abstract; 240 words)
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
Malicious beacon nodes corrupt the position estimates of nodes that never
exchange traffic with them. Prior defences detect them from localisation
evidence and remove them permanently. We study the decision that precedes
removal and what an error costs. A two-stage pipeline fuses behavioural and
localisation evidence into per-observer trust and resolves it into a
three-state verdict; isolation policies are compared across
\PENDING{N} ns-3 runs. First, evidence coverage: each single-evidence
detector confirmed the attacker in 0 of \PENDING{n} trials of the attack
that leaves no signature in its class, while using both confirmed it in
every trial. Second, we introduce cumulative false isolation~$\Phi$, in
node-seconds, which measures how long a mechanism's errors persist. On an
uncontested network, confirmation-gated isolation with release withdrew
4.1\% of honest-beacon service against 73.5\% for the strongest
immediate-isolation policy \PENDING{update to 30 seeds}, at a cost of 15--24\,s of attacker
activity after onset. Third, we derive when weighted Stouffer fusion can
detect what neither branch detects alone: the branches' detection
thresholds must lie within a factor of $1+\sqrt{2}$ for equal weights. Our
measured ratio is at least 2.38, at the boundary, and an 80-run intensity
sweep found no case in which fusion outperformed a disjunctive rule. The
aggregation stage changed no verdict and neither isolation policy produced
a throughput difference exceeding its confidence interval. The campaign is
a proof of concept on one dense topology; Section~8.1 lists the
experiments required before the findings generalise.
\end{abstract}

Elimination of malicious beacons from localisation evidence is well studied
for static beacons. Liu, Ning and Du detect a beacon whose RSSI-implied
distance disagrees with the distance implied by its declared position, and
revoke it once enough alerts accumulate~\cite{liu2005detecting}. Kuriakose
et al.~\cite{embn2023} address the same problem in EMBN-MANET, a UWB-based
mobile ad hoc network, using RSSI and trilateration residuals, and report
complete identification when fewer than four malicious beacons share a
neighbourhood. Both establish that detection from localisation evidence is
tractable. What neither settles is the decision that precedes removal, and
what happens to the network when that decision is wrong: revocation in
both is permanent.

Secure localisation has a longer history in sensor networks. SeRLoc bounds
a node's position using directional beacons without range
measurements~\cite{lazos2004serloc}; verifiable multilateration rejects
position claims inconsistent with distance bounds~\cite{capkun2006secure};
and Liu, Ning and Du detect malicious beacons by the inconsistency between
measured and declared range, revoking a beacon once a base station has
received enough independent alerts~\cite{liu2005detecting}. Our
localisation branch uses the same residual as the last of these. What
differs is the setting --- mobile beacons and commodity 802.11 RSSI rather
than static beacons or UWB ranging --- and the decision rule: our
confirmation is reversible, and we measure what a false confirmation costs
while it persists.

\subsubsection{Calibrating the fused statistic}

The combination rule matters less than how its output is thresholded.
Let $s_B, s_L \in [0,1]$ be evidence scores and $C = \omega_B s_B +
\omega_L s_L$ with $\omega_B + \omega_L = 1$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:convex}
\min(s_B, s_L) \le C \le \max(s_B, s_L),
\end{equation}
so if both scores lie on the benign side of a threshold~$\tau$, so does~$C$.
A detector that applies the single-branch threshold to a convex combination
therefore cannot confirm an attacker that both branches miss. Tables built
this way will still show the fused row ahead of the weaker branch, because
a mean of a strong score and a weak one exceeds the weak one; the apparent
advantage is a property of the comparison, not of the mechanism.

The bound is a consequence of reusing~$\tau$, not of averaging as such.
For two independent standardised branches the arithmetic mean
$\bar{z} = (z_B + z_L)/2$ has standard deviation $1/\sqrt{2}$ under the
null, so a threshold recalibrated to the same false-positive rate is
$\tau/\sqrt{2}$, and the rule ``$\bar{z} > \tau/\sqrt{2}$'' is identical to
the equal-weight Stouffer rule ``$(z_B + z_L)/\sqrt{2} > \tau$''. What the
fusion hypothesis requires is a combined statistic calibrated against its
own null distribution. Weighted Stouffer combination,
\eqref{eq:stouffer}, is unit variance under the null whatever the weights,
so a single threshold serves every configuration and the hypothesis becomes
falsifiable: two branches each at $z=1.5$ combine, with equal weights, to
$Z=2.12$.

This argument disqualifies uncalibrated combination; it does not make
Stouffer's method the best calibrated rule, and we do not claim that it is.
Fisher's method, likelihood-ratio and Bayesian fusion, Dempster--Shafer
combination~\cite{singh2020}, and learned rules can all be calibrated in
the same sense. We chose Stouffer's method because it accepts continuous
evidence without a likelihood model for either branch, admits per-branch
weights directly~\cite{whitlock2005weighted}, and degrades gracefully when
one branch has no observations in a window, which is frequent under
mobility. Section~6.5 compares it against a disjunctive rule; the remaining
rules are listed in Section~9.1.

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
%% Sec 6.3 "Attack Intensity and the Fusion Region"  (replace from
%% "Under that assumption, let theta ..." to the end of the subsection)
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
Under that assumption, let $\theta_B$ and $\theta_L$ be the intensities at
which each branch alone reaches the decision threshold, so that branch~$X$
has anomaly $\theta f/\theta_X$ at intensity~$f$. With effective weights
$\tilde\omega_B, \tilde\omega_L$ the fused statistic of
\eqref{eq:stouffer} crosses the threshold at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ffused}
f_F = \frac{\sqrt{\tilde\omega_B^{2} + \tilde\omega_L^{2}}}
           {\tilde\omega_B/\theta_B + \tilde\omega_L/\theta_L},
\end{equation}
and a fusion-only region --- an intensity at which fusion confirms and
neither branch does --- exists precisely when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:region}
f_F < \min(\theta_B, \theta_L).
\end{equation}
When the branches are equally sensitive, $\theta_B = \theta_L = \theta/a$,
the region is $\theta/(\sqrt{2}a) < f < \theta/a$: a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ in
intensity, half an octave, and the widest it can be.

Our A3 configuration used $f = 0.5$ and found no fusion advantage, so a
single branch at half intensity already crosses the threshold there. Any
fusion-only region must lie below the intensity we tested, which
Section~6.5 examines directly.


%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
%% Sec 6.5.1 "No fusion-only region, and why"  (replace whole body)
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
The predicted region does not appear at any sampled intensity. The reason
is visible in Fig.~4: the two branches are not comparably sensitive.
Behavioural evidence already reaches 8 of 10 detections at the weakest
attack tested, where the attacker drops only 3\% of transit traffic.
Localisation evidence reaches half detection at about 36\,m of displacement
(35.7\,m, interpolated on log intensity).

Writing $r = \max(\theta_B,\theta_L)/\min(\theta_B,\theta_L)$ for the
sensitivity ratio, \eqref{eq:region} with equal effective weights reduces to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ratio}
\frac{\sqrt{2}\,r}{1+r} < 1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
r < 1 + \sqrt{2} \approx 2.414 .
\end{equation}
With unequal weights, let $\tilde\omega_s$ weight the more sensitive branch
and $\tilde\omega_w$ the less sensitive one; the region exists iff
$r < \tilde\omega_w / \bigl(\sqrt{\tilde\omega_s^2 + \tilde\omega_w^2} -
\tilde\omega_s\bigr)$, which falls below $1+\sqrt{2}$ when the less
sensitive branch carries more weight (1.618 at twice the weight) and rises
when it carries less, although the region then narrows towards zero width.
In our configuration the less sensitive branch is localisation, whose
confidence factor saturates nine times faster \eqref{eq:conf}, so the
effective bound is at or below 2.414.

The behavioural branch has not fallen to 50\% at the lowest intensity we
swept, so its threshold is known only to lie below 15\,m and the measured
ratio is a lower bound, $r > 2.38$. The data are therefore consistent with
the absence of a fusion-only region but do not establish it: $r$ sits at
the boundary of \eqref{eq:ratio}, and locating $\theta_B$ requires the
extension below 15\,m in Section~9.1 \PENDING{replace this sentence with
the measured $r$ and its bootstrap CI once STAGE=low has run}. The ratio
also depends on how the sweep couples the two intensities: we scaled
$\delta$ and $p$ by a common factor from $\delta = 300$\,m and $p = 0.6$,
and a different pairing moves both curves along the intensity axis
\PENDING{report the second pairing, STAGE=pair2}. Equation~\eqref{eq:ratio}
is a condition on a configuration, not on the evidence classes in general,
and it can be checked from single-branch detection curves before any
fusion experiment is run.

The seed-level data agree. At the weakest intensity, two of ten seeds
produced failure in both single-evidence branches, and fusion failed on
both as well.

%% Sec 6.9 "False Isolation on an Uncontested Network"  (insert after the
%% first paragraph; numbers from STAGE=phi and STAGE=seeds)

Policy D as defined isolates on the first suspicious window, and in every
seed that window falls at $t = 20$\,s, before any attacker is active. To
test whether the comparison rests on a start-up transient, we evaluated
two stronger immediate policies on the same verdict streams: D$_w$, which
ignores each beacon's first three usable windows, and D$_1$, which isolates
on the first window of malicious evidence (trust below $\tau_M$ with
enough observers in agreement) but without the persistence requirement.
On the clean network both still isolated every honest beacon in every
seed (15/15 node-seeds each), with mean $\Phi$ of 1039 and 882
node-seconds against 49 for policy E. The paired difference between D$_1$
and E is 833 node-seconds (bootstrap 95\% CI [744, 937]); in normalised
form D$_1$ withdraws 73.5\% of honest-beacon service and E 4.1\%. The
advantage therefore does not come from the start-up transient: removing the
persistence requirement alone is enough to isolate every honest beacon.
Table~\ref{tab:phivariants} gives the same comparison under attack, where
every paired interval excludes zero.

For the two attack classes evaluated, single-evidence detection is blind to
attacks outside its evidence class, completely rather than partially, and
collecting both behavioural and localisation evidence removes the observed
